How to choose the right dog harness

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

The best dog harness for you depends on your dog’s size, behavior, and your goals. Look for a harness that offers a secure fit, solid adjustability, and the right control style, front-clip for pullers, back-clip for easy walkers, or dual-clip for versatility. Measure your dog’s neck and girth, not just weight, and prioritize padded materials and reflective details for comfort and safety.

What kind of harness is best for my dog?

The answer comes down to your dog’s leash manners and body shape. A front-clip harness has the leash attachment at the chest; it gently redirects a dog who pulls, making walks more controlled without putting pressure on the throat. These are popular for strong pullers, but some dogs may find the chest strap awkward at first. Back-clip harnesses attach above the shoulders. They’re great for polite walkers or small breeds because they don’t interfere with natural movement. However, they can encourage pulling in dogs that lean into pressure. Dual-clip harnesses give you both options, letting you switch between front and back clips as needed, a flexible choice for training or different environments. Step-in vs. over-the-head: Step-in harnesses are easier for dogs that dislike things going over their head, while over-the-head designs often have more adjustment points for a custom fit. Your dog’s cooperation and your patience matter more than any single style.

How do I measure my dog for a harness?

For a good fit, you need two measurements: the neck circumference (where a collar would sit) and the widest part of the chest/girth, just behind the front legs. Most brands provide a size chart linking these two numbers to a size, but don’t rely on weight alone, a stocky bulldog and a lean greyhound can weigh the same but need very different harnesses. When trying a harness on, you should be able to slide two fingers comfortably under any strap. Too tight and it will chafe; too loose and the dog can back out. Check fit regularly, especially with puppies or dogs that gain or lose weight. A harness that shifts when you pull gently on the leash is too loose. Many retailers offer free returns, so ordering two sizes to try at home is a smart move. Take a video of your dog moving in the harness; you’ll spot any rubbing or restriction better that way.

What materials should I look for?

Nylon webbing is the standard, it’s strong, quick-drying, and affordable. For dogs with sensitive skin or thin coats, look for a soft mesh lining or a padded chest plate. Neoprene or foam padding spreads pressure and prevents chafing on long walks, but can trap heat in summer. Breathable mesh is best for warm climates. Stitching quality matters: double- or triple-stitched stress points (where the leash ring attaches) will hold up longer. Avoid harnesses with thin, scratchy edges. Metal hardware (especially steel D-rings) is more durable than plastic, though plastic clips are lighter and less likely to corrode. For nighttime safety, choose a harness with reflective stitching or strips. Some models also have a built-in handle on the back for lifting or holding your dog steady, very helpful for nervous dogs or quick control near traffic.

Are there safety features I should consider?

Beyond a snug fit, look for a second attachment point, a rear ring for seatbelt clips or a handle for close control. Some harnesses have a breakaway or escape-proof design with an extra belly strap, which is worth considering if your dog is a known Houdini. However, breakaway styles are mainly for dogs that might get caught on something, not for everyday pulling. If you drive with your dog, a crash-tested harness with seatbelt loops adds real protection. These are built with stronger webbing and reinforced stitching to withstand impact. For hiking, choose a harness with a sturdy handle and abrasion-resistant fabric. One often-overlooked feature: a harness that lacks a front chest strap can allow a dog to twist and slip out. Full-body or three-strap designs are more secure than minimalist ones. Always test by gently pulling upward on the back ring, if the harness slides toward the head, it’s not safe for a dog that panics.

Should I choose a harness for a puppy or an adult dog?

Puppies grow fast, so an adjustable harness that lets you extend the straps by an inch or two will save you from buying a new one every month. Many brands offer “puppy” sizes with extra adjustment range. That said, a budget-friendly option that fits well now is better than an expensive “one-size-for-all” that’s too loose and allows slipping. For adult dogs, the priority is durability and long-term comfort. A harness you can machine-wash (remove any metal hardware first) will stay fresh longer. Some harnesses come with a lifetime warranty, a sign the manufacturer trusts its build quality. Senior dogs or those with arthritis may prefer a step-in harness that doesn’t require lifting their front legs overhead. Look for one with wide, cushioned straps and no pressure points over bony shoulders.

Frequently asked questions

Is a harness safer than a collar?

For most dogs, yes, a harness distributes pressure across the chest and back instead of the neck, reducing risk of tracheal injury or eye pressure. Collars are fine for well-mannered dogs on short walks, but for pulling, small breeds, or dogs with respiratory issues, a harness is the safer choice.

How do I stop my dog from pulling in a harness?

A front-clip harness is the most effective tool because it redirects your dog sideways when they pull, discouraging the behavior. Pair it with consistent training, stop walking when the leash tightens, reward when it loosens. No harness works instantly, but front-clip gives you more control without pain.

Can I leave a harness on my dog all day?

It’s not recommended. Even well-fitted harnesses can rub, trap moisture, and cause matting in long-haired dogs. Take it off when your dog is home unsupervised or sleeping. If you need a harness for quick potty breaks, consider a lightweight, minimal design with soft edges.

How do I clean a dog harness?

Most nylon harnesses can be hand-washed with mild soap and air-dried. Check the tag; some are machine-washable on a gentle cycle, but remove any metal clips or patches first. Avoid bleach or fabric softener, which weaken the webbing. Let it dry completely before reattaching hardware to prevent rust.

At what age should I start using a harness?

Once your puppy is fully vaccinated and you start leash walks, you can use a harness. For very small puppies, a lightweight, adjustable harness with soft straps is best. Avoid using a harness for restraint before eight weeks; let them get used to wearing it in short, positive sessions.

Do harnesses cause matting in long-haired dogs?

Yes, especially if the harness has rough nylon straps or is left on for long periods. Look for a harness with a smooth, non-abrasive lining and avoid wide straps that sit directly on the fur. Brushing your dog before and after walks, and removing the harness immediately after, will help prevent tangles.

In shortNo single harness works for every dog, but taking the time to measure carefully, consider your dog’s behavior, and try a couple of well-reviewed models will lead you to one that feels secure and comfortable. Focus on adjustability, quality materials, and safety features that match your daily life, a good harness makes every walk safer and more enjoyable for both of you.
